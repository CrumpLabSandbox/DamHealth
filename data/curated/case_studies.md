## Oroville Dam (CA)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Zoned earthfill embankment; completed 1968; 770 ft tall, the tallest dam in the United States; storage about 3.5 million acre-feet (storage figure is general reference, not confirmed in search results).
- **Owner / regulator:** California Department of Water Resources (DWR), State Water Project; regulated by FERC (hydropower license) and California Division of Safety of Dams (DSOD).
- **What happened or what's at risk:** On 7 February 2017, during heavy releases, a large hole opened in the concrete chute of the main (gated) service spillway and the chute rapidly disintegrated. With the main spillway crippled and the lake still rising, water flowed over the unlined emergency spillway for the first time ever on 11 February 2017. Headcutting erosion on the emergency-spillway hillside threatened to undermine the concrete weir and cause an uncontrolled release; roughly 180,000–200,000 downstream residents were ordered to evacuate on 12 February. Flows over the emergency spillway stopped that evening and the immediate crisis passed.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Spillway chute failure (slab uplift and foundation erosion) driven by inherent design and construction weaknesses, poor bedrock quality under the chute, deteriorated chute slabs and drains, and inadequate repairs over decades; hillside erosion / headcutting below the emergency spillway weir. The Independent Forensic Team (Jan 2018) called it "a long-term systemic failure" of DWR, regulators and industry practice to recognize and address these weaknesses; it noted the principal spillway designer had no prior spillway design experience.
- **Consequences:** No deaths. About 188,000 people (reports range 180,000–200,000) evacuated. Repair and emergency-response costs about $1.1 billion (DWR estimate), up from an initial $200 million estimate; the Kiewit construction contract grew from $275 million to about $630 million.
- **Response and plan status:** DWR demolished and rebuilt the main spillway chute and armored the emergency spillway with a roller-compacted-concrete splashpad, secant-pile cutoff wall and buttress (2017–2019). Work is complete. FEMA initially approved about $333–337 million and in March 2019 denied about $306 million, saying flaws pre-dated the storm; the state appealed, and FEMA later told DWR that the entire main spillway reconstruction and part of the emergency spillway work was eligible, with DWR estimating roughly $750 million of the $1.1 billion as reimbursable (date of that reversal and the final settled amount are unconfirmed). Non-reimbursed costs fall on State Water Project contractors.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Original design flaws and untreated foundation conditions can sit latent for 50 years; periodic inspections that only look at surface condition will not find them. Comprehensive design-basis reviews are needed.
  - An "emergency" spillway that has never been tested is a hazard, not a safety feature; unlined spillway hillsides should be evaluated for erodibility.
  - Disaster-assistance funding (FEMA Public Assistance) may not cover costs tied to pre-existing deficiencies, so owners cannot assume federal reimbursement after a failure.

## Edenville Dam & Sanford Dam (MI)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Edenville: earthen embankment with concrete spillways, built 1924 (some sources 1925), 54 ft high, roughly 6,200–6,600 ft crest length, impounding Wixom Lake on the Tittabawassee and Tobacco Rivers. Sanford: earthen embankment, built 1925, 40 ft high, 6,380 ft crest, impounding Sanford Lake.
- **Owner / regulator:** At failure, privately owned by Boyce Hydro Power. Edenville's FERC license was revoked in September 2018, shifting regulation to Michigan EGLE; Sanford remained FERC-licensed. Both now owned by Midland and Gladwin Counties via the Four Lakes Task Force (FLTF).
- **What happened or what's at risk:** After days of heavy rain, on 19 May 2020 a section of the Edenville embankment slid out and the dam breached, sending Wixom Lake downstream. The flood wave overtopped and breached Sanford Dam hours later. The Tittabawassee crested at record levels in Midland, flooding thousands of structures and Dow Chemical's site.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** FERC's Independent Forensic Team (final report May 2022) concluded Edenville failed by static liquefaction (sudden loss of strength) of very loose, saturated sand in the embankment, which had been built with significant deviations from design plans and specifications. Long-standing inadequate spillway capacity and nearly a century of regulatory and ownership lapses were contributing factors; the IFT called the failure "foreseeable and preventable." Sanford failed by overtopping from the Edenville flood wave.
- **Consequences:** No deaths. About 10,000 people evacuated; roughly 150 homes destroyed; damages exceeding $200 million (one estimate $175 million). Four lakes (Wixom, Sanford, Secord, Smallwood) were drained.
- **Response and plan status:** FLTF is rebuilding all four dams at a total estimated cost of about $350 million, funded by a $200 million state grant, state budget appropriations (including $9.8 million in Oct 2025), and a roughly $200 million special assessment on lakefront property owners (challenged in the Michigan Supreme Court). A $1.4 million FEMA grant was pending as of Oct 2025; an HHPD award is unconfirmed. Sanford Dam was restored and passing water by May 2026 (formal completion projected Feb 2027); Smallwood and Secord expected 2026; Edenville expected September 2027. Work is underway.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - A dam whose spillway capacity is known to be inadequate for decades is an emergency in slow motion; regulators need enforceable tools and funding when a private owner will not or cannot act.
  - Construction records can be wrong: embankments must be characterized by modern geotechnical investigation, not assumed to match 1920s drawings.
  - Transfer of a dam from FERC to a state agency can create a regulatory gap; hand-offs need a dam safety plan and funding attached.

## Spencer Dam (NE)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Run-of-river hydroelectric dam on the Niobrara River: earthen embankment with concrete gated spillway section and powerhouse; original construction completed 1927, major rebuild 1940; about 26 ft high (height is general reference, unconfirmed); small storage with a roughly 3 MW powerhouse.
- **Owner / regulator:** Nebraska Public Power District (NPPD); regulated by the Nebraska Department of Natural Resources (not FERC-licensed). Classified "significant hazard," so no formal EAP was required.
- **What happened or what's at risk:** During the March 2019 "bomb cyclone" flood, a massive ice run and record flows hit the dam on the night of 13–14 March 2019. Ice jammed the spillway gates; the embankment was overtopped and breached, releasing a surge of water and ice that destroyed the home and business of Kenny Angel, 71, immediately downstream, and took out the Highway 281 bridge.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Overtopping and breach of the earthen embankment after ice and flood flows exceeded spillway capacity; ice blocking of gates. The ASDSO independent panel (April 2020) found operators could not have prevented failure that morning, but cited human factors: general ignorance of ice-run risk, underestimation of the downstream hazard (which drove the "significant" rather than "high" hazard rating and the lack of an EAP), warm-season-only inspections that never saw ice conditions, and a 1940 redesign that made it harder to pass ice. The dam had been damaged by ice in 1935, 1960 and 1966.
- **Consequences:** One death (the first U.S. dam-failure fatality since 2006); Angel's home and business destroyed; highway bridge destroyed. NPPD paid $2.5 million to the Angel family; a claim against the state was pending.
- **Response and plan status:** NPPD had already planned to decommission the plant (announced 2015) and decided not to rebuild. FEMA provided $50 million (May 2021) for demolition of the remains and river restoration. Removal/restoration underway or complete (current status unconfirmed).
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Hazard classification should be based on who is downstream and realistic failure modes (including ice), not on normal-weather flow; a mis-rated dam gets no EAP.
  - Inspect under the conditions that threaten the dam, including winter ice, not only in summer.
  - Low-value, aging hydro dams that an owner wants to abandon should be removed promptly rather than left operating with deferred maintenance.

## Rapidan Dam (MN)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Hollow reinforced-concrete gravity dam with Tainter gates on the Blue Earth River near Mankato; built 1908–1910 for hydropower; 87 ft high. Small run-of-river reservoir.
- **Owner / regulator:** Blue Earth County (owner since 1970; previously Northern States Power); hydro operated under agreement by Eagle Creek Renewable Energy (generation stopped after 2019–2020 flood damage). Regulated by Minnesota DNR Dam Safety.
- **What happened or what's at risk:** On 24 June 2024, record flooding combined with heavy debris overwhelmed the dam. Rather than failing at the structure, the river cut around the west abutment, eroding the riverbank, washing away an Xcel Energy substation and later undermining and collapsing a home (the Rapidan Dam Store family's house). Officials declared an "imminent failure condition," but the concrete structure itself did not collapse.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Abutment erosion and flanking (water bypassing the dam around its west end) during an extreme flood with debris blockage; long history of foundation undermining (emergency repairs in 2002, further work in 2010 and 2017) and a 1965 flood that destroyed most of the gates. The county had already completed repair-vs-remove studies showing "doing nothing is no longer a long-term solution."
- **Consequences:** No deaths. One home lost, substation destroyed, County Road 9 bridge compromised; local businesses lost. Dollar damages not confirmed.
- **Response and plan status:** In August 2024 the Blue Earth County Board voted to remove the dam and replace the County Road 9 bridge. Bridge replacement is funded through Federal Highway Administration Emergency Relief and MnDOT disaster assistance (a $5 million FHWA grant announced for flood-damaged roads/bridges including CR 9). The county is pursuing FEMA alternate procedures for the dam removal and working with USACE on river-channel restoration. Barr Engineering won a $1.47 million design contract; demolition scheduled to begin mid-2027 and finish by end of 2028. Dam-removal funding status unconfirmed.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - A structurally sound concrete dam can still "fail" by flanking if abutments and banks are erodible; risk assessments must look beyond the structure.
  - Owners of marginal hydro dams face a repair-or-remove decision; deferring it until a flood forces the issue costs more and removes options.
  - Debris handling and gate operability under extreme flows matter as much as spillway capacity on paper.

## Lake Dunlap Dam (TX)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Low-head hydroelectric dam on the Guadalupe River near New Braunfels, built about 1928–1931 (roughly 90 years old at failure) with three large steel spillgates (about 85 ft long by 12 ft tall each) and a small powerhouse; one of six GBRA Guadalupe Valley hydro dams.
- **Owner / regulator:** Guadalupe-Blanco River Authority (GBRA); now operated for the Lake Dunlap Water Control and Improvement District (WCID). Regulated by Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ) Dam Safety.
- **What happened or what's at risk:** On 14 May 2019 one of the 1920s-era spillgates burst without warning (captured on security video), draining the lake within hours. A similar gate failure had occurred at Lake Wood in 2016. GBRA then announced plans to drain the remaining four Guadalupe Valley lakes because of the risk of further gate failures, triggering lawsuits from lakefront homeowners.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Gate failure: fatigue and corrosion of aging structural steel in the original spillgates with no feasible repair; deferred capital replacement by a small-revenue hydro system.
- **Consequences:** No deaths or injuries. Lake drained for about four years; sharp loss of property values and recreation; homeowners estimated draining all the lakes could cost them $500 million-plus in value. Litigation over who pays for repairs.
- **Response and plan status:** Residents voted (Nov 2020) to create the Lake Dunlap WCID to tax themselves for the project. GBRA secured a $40 million Texas Water Development Board Clean Water State Revolving Fund bond; construction began May 2021 on new hydraulically operated crest gates (about $35 million) with the WCID responsible for debt service and O&M. TCEQ filings record spillgate replacement and embankment hardening completed in 2023 and the lake refilled. Work complete; similar projects at Lakes McQueeney, Placid and others followed.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Mechanical and steel components have finite lives independent of the concrete; gates must be on a replacement schedule, not just an inspection schedule.
  - When one gate of a series fails, assume the sister gates are near failure too.
  - Beneficiaries (lakefront owners) can be organized to fund rehabilitation when the owner's revenue cannot, but it takes years and litigation.

## Taum Sauk Upper Reservoir (MO)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Upper reservoir of a 408 MW pumped-storage plant atop Proffit Mountain; original 1963 kidney-shaped rockfill dike with concrete-faced parapet, about 1.5 billion gallons (roughly 4,350 acre-ft) (built date and storage are general reference, unconfirmed in search results). Rebuilt 2007–2010 entirely in roller-compacted concrete.
- **Owner / regulator:** AmerenUE (now Ameren Missouri); FERC-licensed.
- **What happened or what's at risk:** Before dawn on 14 December 2005, pumps continued filling the upper reservoir after it was already full. Water overtopped the parapet at the northwest corner, eroded the rockfill, and a roughly 600-ft section collapsed, releasing the reservoir in minutes. A 20-ft wall of water swept down the East Fork of the Black River through Johnson's Shut-Ins State Park, carrying the park superintendent's house and family downstream (all survived) and nearly overtopping the lower reservoir dam.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Overtopping leading to erosion/breach of the rockfill embankment, caused by improperly installed and maintained water-level instruments (sensors had come loose from their mounts) and failed backup high-water shutoffs; the reservoir was operated at levels above those the parapet could safely hold; poor original construction and inadequate management attention to dam safety.
- **Consequences:** No deaths; five people injured (superintendent's family). Johnson's Shut-Ins State Park devastated; 408 MW plant offline until 2010. AmerenUE paid a $10 million FERC civil penalty plus $5 million in local improvements (then the largest FERC hydro settlement) and a $177.35 million settlement with the State of Missouri. Rebuild cost about $450 million, largely covered by insurance.
- **Response and plan status:** Reservoir rebuilt as the largest RCC dam in North America (about 2.8–3 million cubic yards) with fully instrumented, redundant level controls; FERC approved return to normal operation 1 April 2010. Work complete, owner- and insurance-funded.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Instrumentation and automated controls are dam safety features and need the same inspection, redundancy and calibration discipline as the structure.
  - Operating with no freeboard margin turns a sensor error into a catastrophic failure.
  - Regulators can use enforcement (penalties, mandated safety programs) to change owner culture after a near-miss or failure.

## Ka Loko Dam (HI)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Earthen embankment plantation-irrigation reservoir on Kauai's north shore, built circa 1890 for sugar irrigation (date is general reference, unconfirmed); approximately 40 ft high, about 400 million gallons (roughly 1,200 acre-ft) at the time of failure (height/storage general reference, unconfirmed).
- **Owner / regulator:** Private owner James Pflueger (purchased 1987); regulated by the Hawaii Department of Land and Natural Resources (DLNR) Dam Safety Program.
- **What happened or what's at risk:** After more than 40 days of rain, the dam breached just before dawn on 14 March 2006, sending a flood down Wailapa Stream that destroyed homes and killed seven people, including a pregnant woman and a toddler. The dam had never been inspected by the state.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Overtopping and breach of the earthen embankment. The state's independent investigation (Special Deputy Attorney General Robert Godbey, Jan 2007) found the emergency spillway had been filled in with earth, probably during grading by the owner around 1997–98, so the reservoir could not pass excess inflow; it also found the state had inadequately funded its dam inspection program and that Ka Loko had never been inspected. The owner's experts argued foundation on alluvial soil made the dam "a time bomb."
- **Consequences:** Seven deaths; homes destroyed; roads and Kuhio Highway damaged. Pflueger was indicted on seven counts of manslaughter in 2008, pleaded no contest to reckless endangering and served seven months (mostly home confinement); large civil settlements followed (amounts unconfirmed).
- **Response and plan status:** Dam not rebuilt; reservoir breached/decommissioned (status general reference). The 2007 Legislature overhauled Hawaii's dam safety law (HB 1905/SB 1945): owners must hold a certificate of approval to impound water, the state gained inspection/entry authority and funding, and about $2 million was appropriated for stabilization, inspections and cost recovery. Funded by state and owner.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Never obstruct or fill an emergency spillway; any grading near a dam needs regulatory review.
  - A dam safety program that exists on paper but cannot fund inspections provides no protection; unregulated "plantation era" and agricultural dams are a nationwide blind spot.
  - Private owners are legally responsible for their dams regardless of what they knew.

## Lake Delhi Dam (IA)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Earthen embankment with concrete gated spillway/powerhouse on the Maquoketa River near Delhi, Iowa; built 1922–1929 for hydropower (general reference); roughly 60 ft high (general reference, unconfirmed); 9-mile recreational lake.
- **Owner / regulator:** Lake Delhi Recreation Association / Combined Lake Delhi Recreational Facility and Water Quality District (a benefited-property taxing district); regulated by Iowa DNR Dam Safety.
- **What happened or what's at risk:** After up to 10 inches of rain in the watershed, the reservoir rose above the embankment crest on 24 July 2010. Water overtopped the earthen embankment next to the concrete spillway, cut a channel through it, and the lake emptied in a few hours; downstream Hopkinton flooded.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Overtopping plus internal erosion/piping of the embankment. The independent panel (Dec 2010) found a concrete core/retaining wall intended to protect the embankment had not been built to crest height, accelerating internal erosion; only two of three spillway gates could be fully opened (one gate malfunctioned), and the spillway capacity was inadequate for the inflow.
- **Consequences:** No deaths. Lake drained for six years; dozens of homes damaged and property values collapsed; damage estimates in the tens of millions (unconfirmed).
- **Response and plan status:** Rebuilt 2014–2016 with a new concrete spillway, gates and embankment at a total cost of about $16 million, funded by a $6 million local bond referendum (over 95% approval), $5 million+ in state appropriations and county contributions. Gates closed June 2016; lake reached its former level in 2017. Work complete.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Verify that as-built protective features (core walls, cutoffs) actually extend to the elevations the design assumed.
  - Every gate must be operable in a flood; one stuck gate can be the difference between passing and overtopping.
  - Recreational-lake communities can finance rebuilding, but only if they organize a taxing district before, not after, the failure.

## Guajataca Dam (PR)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Earthfill embankment with a concrete chute spillway, completed 1929 by the Puerto Rico Irrigation Service; about 120 ft high; Lago Guajataca stores roughly 30,000–37,000 acre-ft and supplies drinking water to some 250,000 people in northwest Puerto Rico (dimensions general reference, unconfirmed in search results).
- **Owner / regulator:** Puerto Rico Electric Power Authority (PREPA); operated for water supply with PRASA; Puerto Rico DNER Dam Safety; USACE involvement under FEMA mission assignment.
- **What happened or what's at risk:** Hurricane Maria (20 September 2017) sent record inflows over the spillway. The concrete chute failed and the spillway and its foundation eroded headward toward the reservoir, damaging the outlet works and the water-supply line. Officials warned the dam was in "imminent" danger of failing; about 70,000 people downstream in Isabela and Quebradillas were ordered to evacuate.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Spillway chute erosion and headcutting under extreme flood flows; aged concrete and unlined/erodible spillway foundation; damaged outlet works limiting controlled drawdown.
- **Consequences:** No dam breach or direct deaths; roughly 70,000 evacuated; prolonged loss of water supply during the island-wide disaster; emergency stabilization cost about $50 million (FEMA-funded).
- **Response and plan status:** USACE carried out emergency repairs in 2017–2018 (lowering the lake, placing stone and concrete barriers, temporary siphons and a new water line), then returned the dam to PREPA; the spillway still posed an unacceptable risk. In June 2023 USACE and PREPA signed an MOA for permanent repairs (new spillway, outlet works) estimated at about $1 billion, funded by FEMA disaster recovery funds through PREPA; a first work order of over $50 million covered risk analysis and engineering (about two years), followed by design and about five years of construction (ENR cited 2028 completion). Current 2025–2026 status unconfirmed; work is underway in design, construction not confirmed started.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Spillway chutes built in the 1920s were not designed for today's extreme storms; chute condition and foundation erodibility deserve the same scrutiny Oroville prompted.
  - Hurricanes compound dam emergencies with loss of power, communications and access; EAPs must assume all three are gone.
  - Permanent fixes for territorial and utility-owned dams can take a decade and depend on disaster appropriations rather than a dedicated dam safety fund.

## Herbert Hoover Dike (FL)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** 143-mile earthen embankment encircling Lake Okeechobee (about 730 sq mi, roughly 1 trillion gallons at typical stage; general reference); built with hydraulic-fill methods and little compaction beginning in the 1930s after the 1926 and 1928 hurricanes killed thousands, raised and extended in the 1960s; crest about 30–45 ft above surrounding land (general reference).
- **Owner / regulator:** U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Jacksonville District (federal dam; USACE Dam Safety Program).
- **What happened or what's at risk:** No failure, but repeated seepage boils, sand boils and piping incidents during high lake stages (notably 1995, 1998, 2004–2005) showed the dike could fail by internal erosion, putting the Glades communities (Belle Glade, Pahokee, Clewiston, South Bay) and parts of the Everglades Agricultural Area at risk. USACE rated it DSAC 1 (very high urgency) in 2006 and held the lake lower than designed for nearly two decades to reduce risk, with hurricane-season evacuation orders issued for lakeside towns (e.g., Irma 2017).
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Internal erosion / piping through the uncompacted hydraulic-fill embankment and its sand/shell foundation; seepage along 32 federal culverts penetrating the dike (identified in 2013 as the greatest single risk); potential overtopping during extreme hurricanes.
- **Consequences:** No failure losses. Constrained lake operations affected water supply, Everglades flows and estuary discharges for years. Rehabilitation cost about $1.5 billion (original estimate $1.8 billion).
- **Response and plan status:** Dam Safety Modification Study (2016) led to a program of roughly 56 miles of cutoff (seepage) wall through the embankment and foundation (contracts including $387 million for 28.6 miles in 2019 and $41.4 million for 4.1 miles in 2020), replacement or removal of all 32 federal culverts, and armoring. Funded by USACE Dam Safety Modification appropriations plus a Florida state contribution (about $50 million, general reference, unconfirmed) and supplemental appropriations. Substantial completion celebrated 25 January 2023, about three years ahead of a mid-2020s target. Work complete; DSAC downgrade status unconfirmed.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Hydraulic-fill embankments built before modern geotechnics need cutoff walls or filters; monitoring alone does not fix piping risk.
  - Pipes and culverts through an embankment are the weakest link; count them and plan for replacement.
  - Dedicated, sustained federal funding (and a state cost-share) let a 15-year program finish early; year-to-year funding would have stretched it to decades.

## Isabella Dam (CA)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Two earthfill embankments (Main Dam about 185 ft, Auxiliary Dam about 100 ft) on the Kern River completed 1953; Lake Isabella stores about 568,000 acre-ft (figures general reference, unconfirmed in search results).
- **Owner / regulator:** U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Sacramento District (federal dam).
- **What happened or what's at risk:** In 2006 USACE found three concurrent deficiencies: seepage/internal erosion through the Auxiliary Dam foundation, inadequate spillway capacity for the probable maximum flood (overtopping risk), and the active Kern Canyon Fault passing beneath the Auxiliary Dam. Rated DSAC 1 (one of the Corps' highest-risk dams), with Bakersfield (population about 400,000) roughly 40 miles downstream. The lake was restricted to about 63% of capacity for more than a decade.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Internal erosion/piping in foundation alluvium; hydrologic deficiency (overtopping); seismic deformation/fault rupture under the embankment.
- **Consequences:** No failure. Years of reduced water storage for Kern River agricultural users and recreation; a $650 million federal program.
- **Response and plan status:** Isabella Lake Dam Safety Modification Project: raised both dams 16 ft, built a new 1,300-ft-wide emergency spillway with a 28-ft-high labyrinth weir (model-tested at Utah State's Utah Water Research Lab), added filters/drains and a seepage cutoff, and relocated Highway 155. Flatiron-led JV construction contract about $323–336 million (reported under budget); total program about $650 million, funded by USACE Dam Safety Modification appropriations. Phase 2 substantially complete November 2022; ribbon cutting 4 April 2023; Phase 3 (visitor center, operations building) followed. Work complete.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - A single dam can carry three independent failure modes at once; risk-informed assessment catches combinations that a spillway-only or seismic-only review would miss.
  - Physical hydraulic modeling of unconventional spillways (labyrinth weirs) can save tens of millions.
  - Interim operating restrictions protect the public but impose real water-supply costs, which argues for funding fixes quickly.

## Anderson Dam (CA)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Earthfill embankment on Coyote Creek near Morgan Hill, built 1950; 240 ft high; Anderson Reservoir is Santa Clara County's largest at about 89,000 acre-ft (figures general reference, unconfirmed in search results).
- **Owner / regulator:** Santa Clara Valley Water District (Valley Water); regulated by FERC (small hydro license) and California DSOD.
- **What happened or what's at risk:** Seismic studies (2009–2016) found the embankment and foundation could liquefy and slump in a large earthquake on the nearby Calaveras or Hayward faults, potentially overtopping and failing the dam and inundating Morgan Hill and San Jose. FERC lowered the allowed storage from 58% to 35% in 2020 and ordered Valley Water to begin draining the reservoir by 1 October 2020 until the dam is rebuilt, saying the risk was "unacceptably high."
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Seismic deficiency: liquefiable embankment and foundation materials, potential crest settlement and overtopping; undersized 4-ft outlet pipe that could not draw the reservoir down quickly in an emergency.
- **Consequences:** No failure. Loss of roughly a third of the district's local surface storage for well over a decade (2020 to about 2034) and heavy ratepayer costs: estimate grew from $576 million (2018) to about $1.9 billion for the retrofit and $2.3 billion for the FERC-ordered compliance program (Dec 2024), and about $2.8 billion total reported in 2026.
- **Response and plan status:** Anderson Dam Tunnel Project (new 13-ft-diameter low-level outlet tunnel, started May 2021) in final stages and expected operational by winter 2026. In July 2026 Valley Water awarded a $1.88 billion construction contract to the Flatiron Dragados Sukut JV to remove and rebuild the embankment; construction to start early 2027 and take about seven years (target about 2033–2034). Funding: Valley Water ratepayers, a $579 million EPA WIFIA loan (Feb 2023), state AB 3005 expedited permitting. Work underway.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Mid-century dams in seismic regions need modern liquefaction analysis; many were designed before the hazard was understood.
  - Interim risk reduction (drawdown) is a legitimate regulatory tool but carries a decade-plus water-supply cost.
  - Cost estimates for major retrofits can grow four- to five-fold between concept and award; budget and finance early.

## Wolf Creek Dam (KY)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Combination concrete gravity section with powerhouse and a long earthen embankment on the Cumberland River, completed 1951; about 258 ft high, roughly 5,700 ft long; Lake Cumberland is one of the largest reservoirs east of the Mississippi (about 6 million acre-ft) with 270 MW of hydropower (dimensions general reference, unconfirmed in search results).
- **Owner / regulator:** U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Nashville District (federal dam).
- **What happened or what's at risk:** The embankment sits on karst (cavernous) limestone. Seepage and sinkholes appeared in the late 1960s and a 1970s grout curtain and diaphragm wall gave only partial relief. By 2005–2007 muddy seepage and new wet areas indicated active internal erosion; USACE declared the dam at high risk of failure (DSAC 1) in January 2007 and dropped Lake Cumberland about 40 ft to reduce pressure. Failure would have flooded Nashville and communities along the Cumberland.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Internal erosion / piping of embankment and foundation soils into solution features in karst limestone (foundation seepage).
- **Consequences:** No failure. Six years of a drawn-down lake hurt the regional tourism economy (losses estimated in the hundreds of millions, unconfirmed); project cost about $594 million.
- **Response and plan status:** Major Rehabilitation (2006–2014): extensive foundation grouting followed by a 4,200-ft-long concrete barrier (cutoff) wall of 1,197 secant piles up to 275 ft deep through the embankment into bedrock, built by Treviicos-Soletanche under a $341.4 million contract (2008). Barrier wall completed March 2013; lake returned to normal levels 2013–2014; project completion summer 2014. Funded by USACE Dam Safety appropriations (and partly by hydropower revenues, unconfirmed). Work complete.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Karst foundations are a lifelong liability; 1970s-era grouting is not a permanent fix and must be monitored for recurrence.
  - Early drawdown bought time without failure; a delayed decision could have been catastrophic.
  - Deep cutoff walls are expensive but proven; costs scale with depth and foundation complexity.

## Center Hill Dam (TN)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Concrete gravity dam with earthen embankment wings and a saddle dam on the Caney Fork River, completed 1948; about 250 ft high; 135 MW hydropower; Center Hill Lake about 2 million acre-ft (dimensions general reference, unconfirmed in search results).
- **Owner / regulator:** U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Nashville District (federal dam).
- **What happened or what's at risk:** Like Wolf Creek, the dam is founded on karst limestone. Seepage through the foundation reached about 130 cfs under normal operations, and sinkholes and wet areas appeared at the main embankment, left rim and saddle dam. USACE rated the dam DSAC 1 in 2007, lowered the lake about 20 ft, and warned that failure would flood communities downstream and contribute to flooding in Nashville.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Internal erosion / piping through solution-widened karst foundation beneath the earthen embankment and saddle dam; left-rim seepage.
- **Consequences:** No failure. More than a decade of lowered lake levels affecting recreation and hydropower; total project cost about $353 million (2006 approved plan was $240 million).

USACE announced completion 1 July 2020 and lake returned to normal operating levels. Funded by USACE Dam Safety Modification appropriations. Work complete.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Grouting first, then a barrier wall, lets the grouting program map the karst and right-size the wall.
  - Saddle dams and rims are part of the dam; they can be the controlling failure mode.
  - A regional cluster of karst dams (Wolf Creek, Center Hill, Dale Hollow) shows the value of screening by geology, not just age.

## Addicks & Barker Dams (TX)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Two long, low earthen flood-control embankments in west Houston (Addicks about 11.6 miles long and 48.5 ft high; Barker about 13.6 miles long and 36.5 ft high) built 1940s to protect downtown Houston from Buffalo Bayou flooding; normally dry reservoirs with combined flood storage of roughly 400,000 acre-ft (dimensions general reference, unconfirmed in search results).
- **Owner / regulator:** U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Galveston District (federal dams).
- **What happened or what's at risk:** In 2009 USACE rated both dams "extremely high risk" (DSAC 1) because of seepage and voids around the original outlet conduits and erosion at the embankment ends. During Hurricane Harvey (August 2017) record rainfall filled both pools to all-time highs; the pools backed up into roughly 10,000 homes built inside the reservoir footprint upstream (land the government never bought), and USACE's controlled releases to protect the dams flooded thousands more homes downstream along Buffalo Bayou.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Internal erosion/piping and conduit deterioration at outlet works (addressed by the modification project); hydrologic/urban-encroachment risk: the government-owned land was smaller than the pool the dams could impound, and development inside and below the reservoirs outpaced the project's design.
- **Consequences:** Harvey: thousands of homes flooded upstream and downstream (plaintiffs estimated property damage over $1 billion); more than 85 lawsuits in the Court of Federal Claims. In December 2019 Judge Lettow ruled the upstream flooding was a Fifth Amendment taking of a flowage easement; downstream claims were dismissed in 2020, reinstated by the Federal Circuit in 2022, and in April 2026 a judge ruled the gate openings made downstream flooding potentially compensable, setting up a damages phase.
- **Response and plan status:** Addicks and Barker Dam Safety Construction Project (2016–2020, about $75 million; Granite Construction $71.9 million contract) built new outlet works with intake towers, steel-lined conduits, stilling basins, cutoff walls and filters; new structures went into service February–March 2020 and the old outlets were grouted and abandoned. Funded by USACE Dam Safety Modification appropriations. Outlet-works work complete; the larger issue of inadequate storage and upstream flooding remains the subject of a Buffalo Bayou and Tributaries Resiliency Study (status unconfirmed), and no project to add storage or buy out encroached land is funded.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - A flood-control dam that works as designed can still cause catastrophic flooding if development is allowed inside its pool; land-use and acquisition are dam safety issues.
  - Downstream release decisions during an emergency carry legal liability; EAPs and public communication need to spell out the trade-offs in advance.
  - Fixing the structure (outlet works) does not fix the hydrology; both must be addressed.

- **Type / built / height / storage:** Earthfill flood-control dam on the Santa Ana River at the Riverside/Orange/San Bernardino county line, completed 1941; raised in the 2000s to about 594 ft crest elevation (about 106 ft above streambed); flood pool roughly 196,000–362,000 acre-ft depending on the spillway configuration (dimensions general reference, unconfirmed in search results).

## Bluestone Dam (WV)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Concrete gravity flood-control dam on the New River at Hinton, WV, completed 1949; about 165 ft high and 2,048 ft long; flood storage about 631,000 acre-ft (dimensions general reference, unconfirmed in search results).
- **Owner / regulator:** U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Huntington District (federal dam).

Downstream are Hinton, Charleston and the Kanawha Valley; USACE described the project as averting a potential catastrophe for hundreds of thousands of people.

Right-side stilling basin completed 2 March 2026; left-side basin is the last task; Phase 5 about 63% complete with completion expected 2030 (said to be five years ahead of plan). Work underway, funded.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Mid-century flood estimates were too low; many concrete dams need stability re-analysis against updated PMFs.

  - A stilling basin is a safety-critical component; if it fails under design flows, the dam's own foundation is next.

  - Flood-control dams that are normally dry can still carry the highest risk ratings when millions live downstream; consequence, not just probability, drives urgency.

## Lewisville Dam (TX)
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Rolled earthfill dam about 125 ft high and 32,000 ft long with a gated concrete spillway on the Elm Fork of the Trinity River, completed 1955; Lewisville Lake stores about 2 million acre-ft total (flood plus conservation) and supplies water to Dallas and Denton (dimensions general reference, unconfirmed in search results).
- **Owner / regulator:** U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Fort Worth District (federal dam).
- **What happened or what's at risk:** A July 2005 assessment identified seepage with potential for piping through the embankment and foundation, plus uplift and sliding stability concerns at the spillway. The dam was placed in USACE's high-urgency category (reported as DSAC 2; rating unconfirmed in search results) with more than 400,000 people in the inundation zone, including much of Lewisville, Carrollton, Irving and north Dallas. USACE stresses the dam is "not at risk of failure" but has known deficiencies.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Internal erosion/piping (seepage) in the embankment and foundation; spillway uplift and sliding instability under high pool.
- **Consequences:** None to date; project authorized at about $150 million with current estimate about $159.6 million.
- **Response and plan status:** Phase 1 seepage remediation (filters, relief wells, toe berms) concluded in 2021. Phase 2 spillway modification: $76.5 million contract (2022) to Shimmick Construction for 73 post-tensioned anchors, an upstream geomembrane blanket in the approach channel, and drilled-pier walls replacing the apron slab extension. A July 2026 USACE story says the spillway modifications are nearing completion with operations uninterrupted. Funded by USACE Dam Safety Modification appropriations with some contributed funds. Work nearly complete.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Seepage at a 1950s earthen dam is common; the question is whether filters and relief wells exist to control it before piping begins.
  - Spillway stability (uplift, sliding) deserves re-analysis against modern pool and load assumptions.
  - Keeping a water-supply reservoir in service during construction is possible with phased design.

## McLeod (McLead / Garfield) Flood Control Dam (NM) — 2026 emergency
- **NID ID:** unknown (not confirmed via search; local coverage notes the NID shows no EAP on file for the dam)
- **Type / built / height / storage:** Roughly 20-ft-high earthen flood-control (detention) dam above the village of Garfield in the Hatch Valley, Doña Ana County; one of a set of small arroyo flood-control dams (with Reed Thurmond Dam) managed by the Caballo Soil and Water Conservation District; built date and storage unconfirmed.
- **Owner / regulator:** Caballo Soil and Water Conservation District (local owner/operator); regulated by the New Mexico Office of the State Engineer Dam Safety Bureau.
- **What happened or what's at risk:** On 29 September 2026, rain from the remnants of Hurricane Polo and a cut-off low overwhelmed the Hatch Valley. The McLeod dam overtopped and the county reported a "piping failure" that kept water from passing through the outlet; the National Weather Service issued a flash flood emergency warning of imminent dam failure, and Hatch, Salem, Placitas, Garfield and Rincon were ordered to evacuate. Crews pumped stormwater downstream to relieve the dam; it did not breach, and most evacuation orders were lifted that night.
- **Root causes / failure mode:** Overtopping from insufficient spillway capacity, plus a piping/outlet failure; a 2023 state inspection had already flagged insufficient spillway capacity, lack of maintenance and signs of erosion, and no Emergency Action Plan was on record.
- **Consequences:** No deaths reported. Several thousand residents evacuated; valley-wide flood damage initially estimated at at least $17 million; about $1 million in damage to the McLeod and Reed Thurmond dams; Doña Ana County declared a flood disaster.
- **Response and plan status:** Emergency pumping and temporary repairs; county disaster declaration and request for state/federal assistance (FEMA status unconfirmed). Permanent rehabilitation unfunded as of October 2026; state editorial calls for a statewide review of small flood-control dams.
- **Lessons for other dams:**
  - Small "low" or "significant" hazard flood-control dams above villages can still force mass evacuations; hazard ratings and EAPs need to reflect who is downstream today.
  - Inspection findings (insufficient spillway, erosion) without a funded corrective-action deadline are warnings, not fixes.
  - Soil and water conservation districts and other small owners usually lack the money to rehabilitate; state revolving funds or FEMA HHPD grants are the only realistic path.

## National facts

- **ASCE 2025 Infrastructure Report Card, Dams: D+** (up from D in 2021). ASCE attributed the improvement largely to one-time IIJA funding. The chapter counts more than 92,000 dams in the National Inventory of Dams with an **average age of 64 years**; **more than 16,800 high-hazard-potential dams** (chapter text; the fact sheet says more than 16,700 and ASCE's 2026 WRDA priorities say "nearly 17,000," about 18% of all dams). As of 2024, **2,522 high-hazard dams (about 15%) were rated poor or unsatisfactory**. ASCE estimates **$185 billion** is needed for dams between 2024 and 2033.
